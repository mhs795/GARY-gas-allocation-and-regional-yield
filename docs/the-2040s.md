# What happens in the 2040s, and why GARY and the GSOO disagree

*Read this before quoting any GARY number after about 2040.*

GARY exports about 920 PJ of LNG in 2045, 453 PJ in 2046, and nothing from 2047.
The 2026 GSOO has the trains running at 1,000 PJ a year right through. This page
explains what is driving GARY, what the GSOO is actually doing, and which of the two
you should believe about what.

Short version: **they are answering different questions, and neither is wrong.** The
GSOO counts gas that exists. GARY asks whether it pays to drill it.

---

## 1. The one sum that decides everything

Under netback pricing the LNG trains are not must-serve demand. They bid. Each one
lifts gas only while the export price beats what the gas costs delivered to its gate:

```
        what a cargo earns          netback, from ACIL Allen
    −   pipeline to the plant       feed-pipe tariff
    −   cost to produce the gas     AEMO's published field cost
    =   margin
```

In the 2040s, Queensland's *marginal* molecule — the last one you have to go and get —
is **Surat 2C**: AEMO's undeveloped contingent resource, published full cost **$6.65/GJ**,
which includes drilling, the processing plant, royalties, tax and a return on capital.
AEMO says so explicitly in the G26 workbook.

So for 2050:

| train | feed pipe | delivered cost | netback | margin |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| GLNG | $0.754 | $7.40 | $7.12 | **−$0.28** |
| APLNG | $0.784 | $7.43 | $7.12 | **−$0.31** |
| QCLNG | $1.417 | $8.07 | $7.12 | **−$0.95** |

**Every train is under water, so the model stops exporting.** That is the whole
mechanism. There is no scarcity trick and no bug behind it — set every scarcity rent
in the model to zero and the same three numbers come out.

## 2. Why it's 2C's cost that matters, not 2P's

Queensland has two grades of gas in GARY, which is how AEMO publishes them:

| | how much | what it costs | what it is |
|---|---|---|---|
| **2P — developed** | 28,911 PJ | $3.65/GJ | wells drilled, plant built |
| **2C — contingent** | 23,270 PJ | $6.65/GJ | known gas, not yet drilled |

The cheap grade pays handsomely at a train — $2.05 to $3.61/GJ of margin in every
year of the horizon. But there is a finite amount of it, and at roughly 1,300 PJ a
year it is gone by 2047. From then on, anyone wanting another GJ has to pay $6.65 for
it.

So **the export question in the 2040s is not "is there gas?" — there are 21,000 PJ
still in the ground. It is "does new gas pay?", and at ACIL Allen's Step Change
netback the answer is no, by 28 cents.**

Three things follow that are worth holding on to:

* **Domestic buyers keep the gas.** Brisbane's alternative is imported LNG at $12–16
  landed, so Brisbane will happily pay $7.35 for gas an exporter won't pay $7.40 for.
  The model doesn't strand Queensland gas — it redirects it. Surat 2C runs at 274
  PJ/yr serving Queensland right through to 2050.
* **The trains go in tariff order**, not all at once. QCLNG, on the dearest feed pipe,
  is squeezed first — it thinned from 2037 and was gone by 2043 on the pre-input-fix
  run — while APLNG and GLNG run on to 2046. Which train loses is set by §7.
* **The Surat price settles at exactly $6.65** from 2047 — the cost of the only grade
  left, with nothing on top. That is the textbook answer to a resource running out,
  and GARY produces it without being told to.

## 3. What the GSOO is doing instead

Four things, and the last is the one that resolves the disagreement.

**It doesn't optimise.** The export volume isn't a GSOO result. AEMO asks the LNG
exporters what they intend to ship and puts that in as demand. Nothing tests whether
shipping it is worth doing.

**Its supply is mostly uncommitted by the 2040s.** AEMO sorts Queensland production
into Existing & Committed, Anticipated, and **Uncertain**. In 2026 the Uncertain share
is 0.3%. By 2045 it is **42%** — 426 PJ of 1,018. The "no supply gap" headline in
Figure 45 rests on development nobody has committed to.

**Cost and price never meet.** The netback lives in ACIL Allen's price report. The
production forecast is a separate exercise. Nothing in the GSOO puts them in the same
calculation. GARY is the first thing that does, which is why it is the first thing to
notice they don't clear.

**And the GSOO stops in 2045.** This is the big one. Add up AEMO's own northern
production: **31,870 PJ over 2026–45**, against Bowen/Surat 2P of about 29,100 PJ. So
their forecast only just crosses out of the cheap gas, in its final year or two — and
then it ends.

> **Every year where this bites is a year AEMO never published.** The "GSOO keeps
> exporting to 2050" that GARY is being compared against is GARY holding AEMO's 2045
> number flat to fill its own horizon. AEMO has no published view of 2050.

## 4. So which do you believe?

| about | believe | because |
|---|---|---|
| **how much gas is there** | the GSOO | it's a reserves inventory, and it's AEMO's own data that GARY uses |
| **is it worth drilling at an export price** | GARY | nothing in the GSOO asks |
| **2046 onward** | neither, yet | AEMO didn't publish it; GARY's answer turns on §5 |

## 5. The number that actually decides it

The export netback is the Asian LNG price less an **avoidable liquefaction and shipping
deduction**. Neither ACIL Allen nor the ACCC publishes that deduction — both treat it
as commercial-in-confidence. GARY uses the midpoint of the public US$1.5–2.5/mmbtu
range: **A$2.872/GJ**, which at ACIL Allen's own exchange rate and heat content makes
the band **A$2.154 – A$3.590**, or plus and minus 72 cents.

The gap to close is 28 cents.

Re-solving the whole horizon with the deduction at the bottom of its own published
range, and nothing else changed:

| year | GSOO | deduction at midpoint | deduction at low end |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2040 | 1,135 | 981 | 1,439 |
| 2043 | 1,000 | 976 | 1,439 |
| 2045 | 1,000 | 921 | 974 |
| 2046 | 1,000 | 453 | 974 |
| 2047 | 1,000 | **0** | **974** |
| 2050 | 1,000 | **0** | **902** |
| **total 2025–50** | 30,427 | 25,750 | **33,645** |

Exports run to 2050, and the horizon total lands **11% above** the GSOO path — the trains
fill their spare liquefaction capacity in the 2030s and 2040s rather than merely meeting
planned volume. Zero shortage in both runs.

> **The export tail is not a model result. It is an assumption about avoidable
> liquefaction cost wearing a model's clothes.** Quote it as a range or not at all.
> GARY's own parameter sheet already says as much: "a CHOICE, NOT A SOURCE — the first
> number to test if a netback result matters."

## 6. What has been fixed, and what it was worth

Three rounds of work in September 2026. None of it changes §1 — the cost-versus-price
sum survives all of it — but each round was removing something that made the answer
wrong for a *different* reason.

| | total exports 2025–50 |
|---|---|
| as shipped | 23,127 PJ |
| + the 2C tranche allowed to feed the trains | 23,127 PJ *(nothing here; **+5,968 PJ** in LNG High)* |
| + terminal value struck on routes that exist | 24,656 PJ |
| + corridor inputs taken from the Bulletin Board | **25,750 PJ** |
| GSOO path | 30,427 PJ |

**The backfill couldn't reach a train.** A filter meant to keep reserved gas out of the
export stream was written on the wrong column and excluded Surat 2C — 23,270 PJ —
from the three feed pipes. Costless in the central case, because 2C is out of the
money there anyway. Worth 5,968 PJ where the netback is high enough to want it: LNG
High used to collapse to 55 PJ/yr by 2049 and now holds full volume to 2050. See
[TODO item 20](../TODO.md).

**Gas left in the ground was valued at a price it couldn't reach.** The terminal value
credited Surat gas at southern import parity less the *tariff* south, and never asked
how much could go — 349 TJ/day of corridor against 6,300 of deliverability. Fixing it
took Surat's backfill tranche from carrying $1.32/GJ of phantom rent to zero, which is
what had been holding the replacement gas *dearer* than the gas it replaces. See
[TODO item 21](../TODO.md) and [`scarcity-rent.md`](scarcity-rent.md).

**The export ceiling was a demand number.** `lng_nameplate_tj_day` = 3,680 TJ/day
started life as the figure the LNG demand trace was scaled to, and got reused as a
physical limit when netback pricing needed one. It was below what the trains actually
did in 2024 (3,942 TJ/day) and below GARY's own 2025 input (3,757). Now read per
facility from the Bulletin Board and capped at the best availability on record. Two
feed-pipe capacities were off their GBB rating as well.

## 7. The one thing still unresolved in the inputs

The three feed pipes run the same corridor — 431, 449 and 468 km by their real routes —
and are priced two different ways:

| pipe | length | tariff | per 1,000 km | basis |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| GLNG_Pipe | 431 km | $0.754 | $1.75 | GARY's network-wide posted median |
| APLNG_Pipe | 449 km | $0.784 | $1.75 | GARY's network-wide posted median |
| WGP_Pipe | 468 km | $1.417 | **$3.03** | AEMO's own posted tariff |

AEMO publishes a tariff for one of the three. GARY carries it, and fills the other two
at the network median rate, which is the documented convention for an unposted arc —
but inside a single corridor it produces a 1.7× spread with no physical reason behind
it.

This is not academic. It is **why QCLNG is the first train to die in every single
run**, and it is the whole of the pre-2046 shortfall against the GSOO. It cannot be
settled from the data in the repo: if the three are really comparable, that gap
largely closes; if WGP really is the dear one, then QCLNG dropping out first is a
genuine result worth stating deliberately. `tests/audit_inputs.py` §12 raises it as a
standing warning so it doesn't get forgotten.

## 8. What would have to change to export like the GSOO

In rough order of how defensible each one is:

1. **The netback deduction moves inside its own published range.** Closes it entirely,
   measured. Not a change to the model — a change to a number that was never more than
   a midpoint. *(§5)*
2. **The two unposted feed-pipe tariffs get a real source.** Closes most of the
   2037–45 gap. *(§7)*
3. **Exports are forced rather than chosen** — foundation contracts assumed to
   recontract past 2036 instead of running off to zero. Reproduces the GSOO by
   assumption, and would tell you what that assumption costs the domestic market. An
   honest scenario, clearly labelled; not a default.
4. **The reserves or the $6.65 get adjusted.** Don't. Both are AEMO's own, and moving
   them to make an export path look right is fitting the model to a conclusion.
